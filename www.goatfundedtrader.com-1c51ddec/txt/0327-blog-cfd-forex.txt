A CFD is a private deal with a broker to exchange the difference between the opening and closing price of a currency pair, such as EUR/USD. Traders post a margin to open a position, which requires a larger capital commitment. Their profit and loss (P&L) fluctuates with pips in the spot market. A small move against a highly leveraged position can quickly erode your margin, leading to forced liquidations or emotional mistakes. You should treat leverage like a power tool: it's helpful when you know exactly what you're doing, but it can cause significant problems when you don't. The cold statistic from Seacrest Markets, "75% of retail CFD accounts lose money," indicates that beginner mistakes, poor risk management, and excessive exposure are usually to blame, rather than just bad luck in the market.How do costs and execution affect outcomes?Spreads, slippage, and overnight financing can significantly erode returns. Major currency pairs typically have tight bid-ask spreads due to competition among banks and liquidity providers. On the other hand, minor pairs tend to widen and experience greater slippage during thin trading hours or when news breaks. Think of this feature as a lever, not free money, and always size each trade based on your free margin and a fixed percent risk for each trade. To determine the position size, consider the account balance you are willing to risk, the stop distance in pips, and the position's pip value. Also, maintain a running free margin buffer equal to at least one full average trade size plus a slippage allowance. Fix these issues by treating your strategy as an experiment: define your hypotheses, enforce out-of-sample validation, and simulate real fills before scaling.How can backtests fool you?When you tune multiple parameters using the same historical window, you inadvertently introduce noise into your results, making it appear as if it were skill. Lookahead bias, survivorship bias, and repeated parameter fitting can create a chart that looks perfect on paper but fails in live conditions. To avoid this, use rolling, walk-forward tests and strict out-of-sample windows. Also, log every parameter change to measure how much performance depends on the test set rather than a real edge.Which performance numbers should you trust?Rely on a basket of measures, not just one main number. Compare compound growth, expected per-trade return, and the worst drawdown sequences. Also, check the distribution's shape, skew, and tail behavior. Run bootstrap resampling on trade-level returns to see how often the edge you see could happen by chance. Report confidence intervals instead of single estimates. This way, you can determine whether a high Sharpe ratio is sustainable or just a result of good luck.How much market friction should you bake in?Assuming zero slippage and flat spreads will lead to inaccurate predictions in live trading. It’s essential to model variable spreads, commission structures, partial fills, and market impact for the specific pair and session your system targets. A practical approach is to replay historical ticks when available. Alternatively, consider adding conservative slippage floors based on the worst 25 percent of fills you’ve observed. This evaluation can help you reassess expectancy and drawdowns under these conditions.What are the risks of quick backtests?Most traders test their strategies with quick backtests because they are fast and easy. Push the system through past crises and times of low liquidity, while ensuring stability across at least two very different market conditions.
