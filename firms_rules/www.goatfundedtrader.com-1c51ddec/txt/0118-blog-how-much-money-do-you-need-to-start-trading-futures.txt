Aim to fund enough capital to cover initial margin plus a meaningful buffer so you can manage stop losses, commissions, and ordinary market noise without panic.How little can you technically start with?Many retail platforms advertise low entry points. A standard listing is a "$500 minimum deposit", which is a marketing number for opening an account, not guidance for trading size or risk management. Treat that figure as a door fee, not your working capital.What range do experienced traders use?Community estimates cluster around a practical starter window, often quoted as "$500 to $5,000", because different strategies and contract sizes change the math. When you trade at the absolute minimum, a routine adverse swing triggers a margin call or forced liquidation before you can respond, and that pressure breeds bad decisions, not better trading. In short, the minimum buy experience does not buy endurance.How should you size your starting bankroll?If your goal is to build skill instead of just testing the mechanics, treat margin as the floor and provide 2 to 3 times that amount per active contract as a buffer. That lets you set sensible stop losses, handle a string of small losses, and still follow a 1% or smaller risk-per-trade rule. Think of it like packing for a trip: the airline allows you to travel with a single carry-on, but if you want to arrive ready, you bring a checked bag too.What costs do small accounts forget?Commissions, exchange and regulatory fees, slippage, and occasional hardware or platform hiccups compound quickly when your capital is tight. Even low-cost micros with sub-dollar round-trip fees can erase a run of small winners if you overtrade to chase gains. Account friction is not dramatic; it accumulates silently until the account no longer functions as a learning vehicle.Most traders start with the familiar shortcut, and it makes sense — it is the cheapest path into live markets. That mix means your usable runway can be 10 to 30 percent smaller than your nominal account balance on a bad month. Treat that as a sizing test, not a rule; run the numbers for your average loss, apply a buffer, and ask whether you can still follow your plan if the market moves against you for weeks.What do smart traders do differently from the start?They make execution and contingency plans explicit. That means rehearsing worst-case fills, building a liquidity map for each contract and session, and keeping a reserve to cover margin spikes during scheduled macro events. Think of capital like fuel in a long rally car stage: enough for the route, plus reserve for the unexpected, otherwise you are driving to the side of the road and learning nothing helpful. Exchanges set the initial and maintenance margins that determine how many contracts you can run at once, and that directly limit your position-sizing choices. Because some contracts have very low posted requirements, HighStrike Trading notes that "Margin requirements for futures trading can be as low as 5% of the contract value." This pattern appears across retail and funded accounts: traders feel frustrated when margin swings reduce active positions and force premature stops, and that emotional squeeze degrades decision-making faster than any single losing trade. Brokerage and exchange fees are not just line items; they change the break-even point for every strategy. According to HighStrike Trading, "The average commission for trading futures is $2.50 per contract." That per-contract fee is the baseline drag you must cover before your system shows net gains, and round-trip costs add up quickly for high-frequency work. Calculate your effective cost per round-trip, add a realistic slippage allowance, then express that total in ticks against the contract's tick value to see whether your average win covers it. If it does not, shorten your timeframe, change contract size, or switch to sessions with better liquidity.What practical test separates theoretical edge from real profitability?  Run a one-week cost audit before you scale: record commissions, exchange and regulatory fees on every fill, note fills that missed your limit price, and convert all costs to ticks. Over multiple sessions, compare that tick-cost distribution to your historical trade outcomes. If your median trade loses more ticks to friction than it gains from edge, you are trading the wrong instrument or the wrong time.
