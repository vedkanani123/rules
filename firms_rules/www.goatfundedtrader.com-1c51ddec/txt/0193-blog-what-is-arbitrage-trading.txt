You buy where it's cheaper, sell where it's more expensive, and pocket the spread before the gap closes. The strategy works because markets aren't perfectly efficient. Information travels at different speeds, regional demand varies, and temporary imbalances create brief windows where identical assets trade at different prices.The appeal is straightforward: you're not betting on whether an asset will rise or fall. You're exploiting an existing pricing error. When a stock trades at $50.20 in Frankfurt but $50.00 in Tokyo, that $0.20 difference is yours if you can execute both transactions before the market corrects itself. The risk feels minimal because you're not exposed to directional market movement. You're simply catching inefficiencies as they appear.How the Mechanics Actually WorkThe process starts with surveillance. You monitor multiple exchanges or platforms simultaneously, watching for the same financial instrument, whether it's a currency pair, commodity, or equity, displaying unequal values. The moment you spot a discrepancy, you execute two nearly simultaneous transactions: purchase at the lower-priced venue and sell at the higher-priced one.Speed determines everything. Even a 500-millisecond delay after the last touch event can mean the difference between capturing a spread and watching it evaporate. Automated trading systems now dominate this space precisely because human reaction time can't compete with algorithmic execution. By the time you manually place both orders, the price gap has likely narrowed or disappeared entirely.This simultaneous buying and selling serves a broader market function beyond your individual profit. Each arbitrage transaction pushes prices toward equilibrium. Your buy order lifts the lower price slightly; your sell order pressures the higher price downward. Multiply this across hundreds of participants and thousands of transactions, and you're witnessing how markets self-correct. The strategy improves pricing accuracy across global exchanges, even as it pursues personal gain.Why Pricing Gaps Exist at AllEconomic theory suggests efficient markets should eliminate these opportunities instantly. Perfect information flow would mean identical assets always trade at identical prices. Reality doesn't cooperate. Data transmission delays, regional trading-hour differences, and varying liquidity levels across exchanges create temporary mismatches. A piece of news might reach New York milliseconds before Singapore, creating a brief window when prices haven't yet aligned.Currency conversion adds another layer. An asset priced in euros on one exchange and in dollars on another can create exchange-rate fluctuations that temporarily distort relative values. Transaction costs also play a role. If moving money between exchanges incurs a 0.3% fee, but the price difference is only 0.2%, the arbitrage opportunity exists on paper but not in practice. Many apparent gaps vanish once you account for fees, spreads, and conversion costs.The familiar approach for traders with limited capital is watching these opportunities pass by, knowing the math works, but lacking the resources to act.
